### “The Rant”

Anyone that hangs around me long enough will doubtless hear “the rant.” The rant knows no limits—social niceties, cultural conventions, and sidelong eyerolls cannot stop it. The rant has known many settings, late night phone calls and long car rides are a favorite, but for the most part it is indiscriminate and opportunistic. To be fair to myself, everyone has a rant of some kind. I’m sure you can think of one you’ve idly rehearsed in the shower, or perhaps you’ve made it as far as subjecting your relatives to it at a family barbecue, not that I would know.

My rant is about technologies of attention: what they are, what they do to our perception of and interactions with the world, and the system that is forming as we, at an increasingly rapid rate, fuse with them. I know, it all sounds very dramatic, and believe me, it really is. Simone Weil, keenly picking up the threads laid down by millennia of thought, concludes that “attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity.” So why do we give it away with so little care?

One aspect of the rant that has intrigued me over the last few years is the transformation in how it is received. In the early iterations, it was a truly niche topic in my social circles and only garnered conversations with likeminded folks, people working in tech, or those who had made infosec and privacy into a hobby of sorts. Tellingly, as we have slid further into the post-COVID technological landscape, there has been an unmistakable tone shift. A topic that once felt unrelatable, even alienating at times, has gone somewhat mainstream.

### Cottage-core and its consequences

I certainly cannot take any credit here, despite it being a special interest of mine, enormous social forces have been at work. At the height of the pandemic, the cottage-core aesthetic captured the imaginations of many a locked down, screen-bound, freshly minted remote worker. It seemed like overnight, everyone was baking their own bread, churning their own butter, or taking up some kind of needlework project. There was a palpable craving in the zeitgeist at the time for any kind of embodied experience. Its not surprising, and only a touch ironic, that this occurred during (and due to) the greatest expansion of social media use since the late aughties boom.

As we gradually reentered the world, cottage-core received a bit of an overdue reckoning. Despite starting as an idle lockdown fantasy, I sense that many came to believe in it, in a way. An ideal that had been born out of a moment of deep technological isolation gave voice to a serious yearning for direct experiences. It had surpassed fantasy into escapism, and even beyond for some. If it had stayed in the realm of fantasy, I’m not sure people would have been so disappointed when the proverbial shoe dropped. All those people churning butter began to realize that churning butter was hard! How were those influencers doing it anyways? That and sewing their own clothes, tending to their sheep, making their own organic bubblegum, and somehow finding the time to set up professional photoshoots to capture it all? It seemed that every week there was another influencer exposé reel circulated. It was props, it was a set, it was a trust fund. It was inevitable that reality would again return to the realm of fantasy, just a regression to the mean.

While I personally didn’t follow the cottage-core scene very closely, I certainly identified with the social discontent it was speaking to. In a world of ceaseless zoom calls, outdoor “pod” hangouts, and hours spent rotting on Instagram, any depiction of an alternative was intoxicating. Its enormous popularity was testament to the presence of what it appealed to. It didn’t so much speak for itself in the end, as much as for the epiphenomenon that it harnessed. And maybe I’m giving the influencers too much credit here, perhaps they didn’t have us all so fooled—maybe it was a somewhere in between fiction and deception, in that weird space of kayfabe where we all nodded our heads in affirmation, but privately wanted more than the illusion could bear.

### A bit more on attention

People that know me in real life, and now I suppose anyone that stumbles on this blog post, also knows that I am a practicing Buddhist, and practicing attention tends to come with the territory. Of course like any practice, there are high tides and low tides, and while I will never claim to be any better or worse at it than anyone else, there is one thing my experience has made clear: attention changes the world. This wisdom is expressed continuously by the teachers of the dharma, reframed by a number of western thinkers such as Weil and presently McGilchrist, and distilled by poets like Mary Oliver and W.S. Merwin. It is also perhaps the most immediately accessible truth available to anyone; after everything else has been stripped away, we still have the ability to choose what we attend to. This is sadly so fundamental that it often goes ignored rather than exercised. Many (including myself) take it as a matter of course that attention is a thing, at least mostly, out of our control. Yes, you can wrestle it towards a task for a limited time, but it all too quickly flies away to some unknown destination. This is all the more true in our terminally distracted world. What many people don’t know, or have forgotten, or don’t have the space to realize is that attention is both a (*the*) way of being in the world and something that grows stronger with practice. It is perhaps *the only* skill that grants access to the greater phenomenological world.

I think anyone who is being honest with themselves has experienced the frustration of barely being able to sit through a movie without feeling restless. I’m not even talking about a 7-hour slow cinema marathon like *Sátántangó*, just a garden variety Hollywood blockbuster will do. There is this pressing urge to get up, if only mentally, to be somewhere else. But almost always it is to check the phone. “Did I miss a text?” “I think I felt a buzz a second ago.” “Let me see where the Doordash driver is.” Are all entryways to the world of the phone. Pretty soon you’re flicking through Instagram reels, not even sure how you got there and unable to describe the last ten you’ve watched, and the movie you were watching has flown by you, and even with the subtitles on you’ll never catch up to what’s going on now. The temporary distraction becomes the center of attention, and it is devilishly good at doing it. Once you start tracking this pattern, the power these devices have becomes undeniable. How many thousands of times did I receive a notification, something that should have perhaps warranted a 60 second lapse of attention, and find myself in a 15 minute deep wormhole on some other app. The phone is labyrinth with many entryways but only a single exit. It was designed that way to harvest the most precious resource on Earth.

There is also a deep frustration in knowing that things haven’t always been this way. You don’t even have to go back to the storied 1990’s, 2010 will do just fine. If you’ve been alive for 20 to 25 years, that is probably enough to have witnessed the shift. Typically this discussion is far too exclusionary of younger people and is reserved for bandying about in Xennial circles, also wont to brag about “drinking the hose water” and “coming home when the streetlights came on.” Of course there was a steadily growing technological presence in the home through the late 90’s. Without betraying my age too much, it was quite miraculous when booting up the family computer did not require entering an MS-DOS prompt. The early 2000’s saw the adoption of Blackberries, almost quaintly referred to as “Crackberries” for their attention grabbing email feature. Palm Pilots promised the digital world but gave you a clunky stylus to stab at a terribly unresponsive screen. It wasn’t until the introduction of the iPhone in 2007 that the tidal wave began to take shape.

Enough ink has been spilled on the topic of the iPhone to permit me to basically skip this entire epoch, we all know the story anyways. The only thing I will establish is the fact that what the iPhone delivered was smoothness. It was many things but above all, it was the first *good* piece of technology. Simple, effortless, it “just worked.” It also was the first device to truly extend the digital world to our pockets. Carrying around an iPhone was like having a personal portal to the internet. Sadly, it did not take corporations long to realize the crucial fact about portals: they go both ways. The final thing I will add to the iPhone discussion is that it didn’t happen overnight. Yes, the iPhone was literally an overnight success in every sense of the term. They flew off the shelves and it was a matter of days from the launch that you saw them in the wild. But for many, especially folks who could not justify the price tag, they remained a gadget for the tech-minded crowd for years. Even for those early adopters, I think there was still a delay between actually having the device in your pocket and learning how to use it properly. By this I of course mean learning how to use it properly on Apple’s terms. People had not yet learned how to fuse with the technology and the world had not yet begun to mold itself to it. Despite its frictionless user experience, it was still an alien device to the world at large. Companies could see the potential of the land, but they hadn’t quite figured out how to sow and reap it yet, and so the adoption of the iPhone was more gradual than I think most narratives recognize.

That was a bit of a detour to say that there are many more people than just Boomers and Xennials that remember the before times. A window of half a generation passed before the “pocket computer” matured enough to be called a “smartphone”, and then just a “phone” again. And so there are many younger people, typically excluded or even used as a prop in this conversation, who *do* remember sitting through a movie uninterrupted, or leaving the house without a way to be contacted, or having to bullshit their way through a conversation without Google to assist. I think there is real grief over this and it doesn’t take much prompting to draw it out. The “hose water” polemic is tired but true. People of all ages are quick to reminisce about their lost attention, even if that isn’t what they call it. I also think people inherently recognize that this change did not happen on their terms. It is like looking around your house for something you knew you had, that you know you just saw, but can’t quite remember when, only to find out that it had been taken rather than misplaced.

I also sense that people have begun to feel a bit Pavlovian lately. It is impossible to interact with modern apps without noticing the automaticity they have trained. I, for my part, had the exact swipe sequence needed to reach Instagram memorized; I probably could have done it with my eyes shut. Even the sequencing of content feels predictable. There is the lure, some interesting short video that leaves just enough to the imagination to warrant a click, then the rabbit hole of increasingly obscure and niche content, finally climaxing in the inevitable hate-watch videos. This plays out over and over again, and often we are left with a “why did I just do that” ringing in our heads. This little gap is where the light gets in. It is the recognition that it really isn’t part of our nature to be this way and it isn’t in our interests to do so. Hiding somewhere in that space between scrolling and the lock button is the recognition that attention is something you train, and if you don’t do it, someone else will do it for you.

### “The experiment”

Where is all of this going? Well I certainly didn’t plan to write a diatribe about cottage-core and iPhones. But I also try to write from the heart with minimal forethought, so I suppose that is where my mind is right now. I originally set out to write about “the rant” and “the experiment” but uncovered a few things along the way. In hindsight, those topics trace part of the chord between those two endpoints, and if they are relatable enough, maybe they can draw that line for you too.

“The experiment” is something I’ve been talking about for a long time, but somehow putting off for even longer. Its a reaction to but also a moderation of the role technology plays in my life. As this post makes pretty clear, I was a pretty heavy phone user. At times it felt like carrying around a pack of cigarettes in my pocket while trying to quit smoking. I had spent a lot of time acknowledging this (see: “the rant”) but very little time doing anything about it. In that intervening time, I have seen my attention degrade precipitously and the Pavlovian feeling creeping in. There was some effect too of realizing that, sometime soon, I’ll have spent more of my life than not with a smartphone. I spent more and more time wondering what I was truly gaining from this arrangement and I began to wonder what a new contract with my technology might look like. For the record, I am not a dogmatic “phone-bad” person. There are many wonderful qualities of these devices that I would not want to lose: GPS, a camera, a flashlight (I think this was the first app developed for the iPhone), a calculator. Many more functions on top of these too are massively enabling. Then what sort of Faustian pact I had made with the rest of the device?

Stafford Beer famously coined the delightful acronym “POSIWID,” the purpose of a system is what it does. What did my personal technological world *do*? Well some of it, the calculator, the camera, the GPS, was *connective*. These were tools that allowed me to interface with the real world more effectively. I know I have gone on [[essays/2026/05-May/False Idol, Dream Virus|rants about “ease” and “smoothness”]] in the past but you’ll have to bear with me, I’m still figuring all this out. A lot of ease and smoothness is quite helpful, doubly so for those with disabilities or the elderly; it would have served to have taken a less totalizing stance on that topic before. But I think the distinction might not entirely live in the ease or smoothness of a technology, but in its *enclosure*. Unlike technologies of connection that are built to be instrumental, technologies of attention are typically built to be ends in and of themselves. What they *do* is trap and confine, any connection they foster is largely incidental to their purpose, or at most a convenient excuse for their existence. As I wrote in my previous piece, they identify a societal need, propose themselves as the solution, and then undermine any alternatives, they are a parasitic architecture of the mind. Interestingly, this is also Cory Doctorow’s “enshittification” at work at an mental and emotional scale. We have offloaded our attention to a system that promised ease, were given enclosure, and now we have to pay to get it back.

It started with a full inventory of my digital life. Everything I do on a device logged and considered. Did this activity feel mostly connective or mostly enclosing? What were the things I truly needed to do on the timeframe of minutes, hours, days, weeks? It turns out that many of the things I felt required the utmost urgency needed nothing of the sort. A good example is email. There are very few emails that are so time sensitive as to require a response in less than 24 hours. I would argue that unless you are in the medical or legal professions, or in an active combat zone, very few things are that life-or-death at all. Anything that could be handled in greater than 24 hours, more than 90% of the time, would be pushed off to my computer—it didn’t need to be in my pocket, so it would stay on my desk. Since I already had the patient open for surgery, I reconsidered my relationship with streaming media as well. Was I okay with suddenly losing access to music or movies that I had paid for? Was I okay with the patterns of consumption that those apps cultivated? My full assessment was in, I had arrived at a plan for technological de-evolution.

I’ll write a different post about how I did this later, but for now I’ll lead with saying that it has been a nearly total overhaul in a very short amount of time. Yes, unfortunately it did require purchasing some different devices (the design of the technology greatly influences how we use it) and has come with some growing pains. I was very careful to avoid turning this exercise into yet another outlet for consumption, as so often happens with these types of projects. The “this next technology will be the one to fix everything” mindset is what got us here in the first place, and more consumption can never be a way out of the consumption trap. I carefully weighed each choice and every chance I had to *not* buy something was taken. Still, I wont say it was as simple as buying a dead-stock Motorola Razr on Ebay. The world has moved on and things that were not in the general consciousness then are necessities now. I’ll save the details for that next post, but I’ve landed on a “dumbphone” (the Light Phone III, which I personally feel has more of a middling intelligence), a restored iPod (the 5th gen one with the clickwheel), and a good old fashioned notepad and pen. So far, that is everything I’ve needed to meet my immediate technological requirements. Anything that falls outside that scope (things in the > 24 hours urgency bucket) have simply been pushed off to my laptop.

I’ll keep checking in here intermittently with my thoughts, struggles, and musings. I’m certain as life moves on, I’ll need to move with it, but I’m trying to do it on my terms.
