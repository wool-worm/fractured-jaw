### Technological De-evolution

In my last post I promised specifics on how exactly I’m going about [[blog/2026/08-Aug/Lockdown Sourdough|establishing this new contract with my technology]]. I alluded to the hardware bits: the phone, the iPod, the notepad. I also spoke to the (relative) difficulty—not just being able to buy a Nokia 3310 and call it a day—but instead weighing the technologies against both the legitimate needs of our current moment and the attention economy that I so badly want to exit. I briefly touched on the *immense* amount of work the switch has taken, particularly in divesting from media streaming. But what none of these reveal on their own, but all point to in concert, is perhaps the most profound thing to come out of this entire exercise: that this project has  been a glimpse into how much control we truly cede to these devices and, by proxy, the corporations that proliferate them.

One sharp reversal I experienced in this process was my sense of the effort involved; maybe more so what that effort *meant* than anything else. That the effort itself was evidence of all the decisions that were made behind the scenes for me. Every minute detail had already been engineered, designed, focus-grouped, and unit tested to its absolute apotheosis before it reached me and to turn away from that and to take back control is face that 

It is impossible to go back in time, nor would I want to, nor would it make sense to. Too much of our context has changed, and things that were not relevant 20 years ago are now commonplace. While there is a valid criticism that we wouldn’t need fancy password managers and hardware encryption keys if the technologies that these themselves were built to protect against did not exist—a kind of arms race—no one is going to put those particular genies back in the bottle. Too many of our advances are genuinely empowering and, used correctly, can support human flourishing. So, as in all things, we arrive at a crossroads of tradeoffs. What parts of this greater context we find ourselves in will we accept into our lives? As empowering? Even as necessary evils? And what parts of this context will be rejected in favor of more humane ones? Saying it is subjective is a truism, some people may want (or need) to make tradeoffs that others may not make. But I would challenge anyone, being honest with themselves, to ask: how does it make me feel when a salvo of red, pinging notifications scroll across my screen? To stop and truly think about what it means, on a lizard brain level, to feel a phantom buzz in your pocket.
