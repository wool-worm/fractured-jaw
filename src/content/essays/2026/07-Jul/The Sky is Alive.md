Have you ever been out on the Mesa or on the ocean and felt like something huge and strange was pushing down on you? Probably not. Most people don’t. Most people get to spend their lives blissfully unaware of how close it is all the time. I know you won’t believe me. None of the other doctors ever have either. I know you will write in your notes “Stella has acute agoraphobia and anxiety disorder.” or whatever and file me away in your drawer. I am telling you the honest truth so that the next person like me might have a warning.

I am not agoraphobic, my parents sent me to a support group one time when I was 18 and everyone there was scared of not being able to escape or crowds or whatever. That isn't the problem, you see? I’m not scared of what’s out in the world, I am terrified of what’s coiled around it, and squeezing tighter.

I grew up on a cattle ranch in New Mexico. The sky in the desert is huge and inexorable, it stretches farther than any single thing should be able to encompass. Seeing it should be beautiful, but I always felt uneasy outside even as a little kid. I got diagnosed with anxiety at six, by ten I stopped going to school. I couldn’t stand the 45 minute bus ride along the flat straight road to the small town nearby. I always felt like the bus was being chased along the highway, like that feeling in a dream of running away from something but everything feels too slow to let you escape…

I could always feel how close it was, pressing down on our little house. I have never had a job, or a boyfriend, or anything. How could I? How am I supposed to think about anything else? How am I supposed to have a normal life? My brothers couldn’t feel it. They loved the ranch. Riding horses and four wheelers over the 300 acre property. Girlfriends, pickups, beer. That was them.

Last month my middle brother John was killed. Died right after my 21st birthday. They said it was the dust storm that had kicked up and hit our little town in a rush. I was in the house. I could feel it building, the force of it pressing in to strike. I hid in the crawl space under the house. I wanted to warn them, I tried–they found my brother the next day, his four wheeler had flipped and pinned him, but that’s not what killed him. The coroner called it “acute pneumonia.” He had suffocated on the dust in the air.

But I knew.

It had found him. Alone on the plains and it had smothered him, slowly crushing the air out of his lungs. It was coming for me next.

So I ran. I took John’s pickup and fled. I don’t know where I thought I was going… How do you escape something that is so encompassing? I could hear it then. The sky. I can still hear it like a bone deep ache, like when you try to press two magnets together, or stand too close to high tension power lines. I can hear the sky as it wraps its teeth around the earth trying to consume it.

I could feel it coiling around the truck and trying to press the air out of my lungs.

The radio said a derecho was moving through the southwest, it was chasing me, after two days of driving I had run out of money, and then out of gas. I was in the desert somewhere, and I ran for the cliffs. I found a fissure in the rock, a sliver of a cave that went maybe 100 feet back. I slid into the safety of stone as the storm hit the cliffs. Have you ever been in a dust storm? The little sliver of light at the entrance of the cave turned dim, the wind lashed in trying to pull me out of the safety I had found. The sound shook my bones inside of my flesh. All I could think about was John. My poor sweet idiot brother--was this what he had felt? Trapped under his four wheeler, the feeling of something so terrible and huge and spiteful. God. Poor John.

They said they found me four days later. I was still hiding in the fissure of rock somewhere outside of Las Vegas. I was dehydrated and unconscious. I had hidden in what passed for a cave trying to get away from it, and I had survived the derecho. After the hospital, that’s when they brought me here.

It doesn’t matter if you believe me, or if you prescribe me the newest anxiety medication. It is still right outside my window. Waiting. Coiled around the earth and this stupid facility. I don’t have long. I will die. Just like my brother. And the coroner will have some reasonable explanation for what happened, but you mark my words. The sky is alive.
